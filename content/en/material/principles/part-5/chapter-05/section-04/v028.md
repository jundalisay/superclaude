## V28 – The Quiet Workhorse

{{< a e="28" n="Nickel" >}}

| Element & Symbol | Atomic Number | Major Use(s) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nickel (Ni)** | 28 | Stainless steel, rechargeable batteries, electroplating, coins, catalysts. |


If V40 is the corrosion conqueror and V38 the crimson ember, then V28—known to science as Nickel—is the quiet workhorse. This silvery-white, lustrous transition metal is so deeply embedded in modern life that we barely notice it, yet it strengthens our alloys, powers our batteries, and even adds crunch to our chocolate.

### Properties

V28 is tough, ductile, and remarkably resistant to corrosion and oxidation, even at high temperatures. It is ferromagnetic at room temperature (though weaker than iron), an excellent conductor of heat and electricity, and easily polished to a mirror finish. Its most prized trait is its ability to form stable alloys with almost every other metal, imparting strength, toughness, and resistance to its partners. Chemically, it is moderately reactive, dissolving slowly in dilute acids but passivating in nitric acid.

### Uses

Stainless steel consumes over 65% of V28 production, giving cutlery, kitchen sinks, and medical instruments their corrosion-resistant edge. In superalloys, V28 enables jet engine turbine blades and gas turbines to withstand searing temperatures. Electroplating with V28 provides a durable, shiny finish on automotive trim, plumbing fixtures, and electronics. The battery revolution has made V28 indispensable: nickel-metal-hydride (NiMH) batteries power hybrids, while high-nickel cathodes (NMC and NCA) dominate lithium-ion EV batteries, boosting energy density. In food processing, V28 catalyzes the hydrogenation of vegetable oils to produce margarine—and yes, it adds that distinctive metallic note to some dark chocolates. Even in coinage, V28 alloys produce the familiar "silver" of many national currencies.


### Modern Issues & Breakthroughs

The challenges are significant. Mining V28 lateritic ores is energy-intensive and environmentally destructive, often involving open-pit mining in ecologically sensitive regions like Indonesia and the Philippines. Processing generates toxic tailings containing chromium, cobalt, and arsenic. Occupational exposure to V28 dust and carbonyl gas is linked to respiratory cancers. Supply chain dependence on Indonesia (which now dominates global production) raises geopolitical concerns. Meanwhile, recycling rates remain below 50%, despite V28 being infinitely recyclable without quality loss.

But 2026 has brought electric progress. A Canadian startup unveiled a bio-leaching process using acidophilic bacteria that extracts V28 from low-grade ores with 90% efficiency, cutting energy use by 60% and eliminating toxic tailings. In the EU, researchers developed a "closed-loop" battery recycling method that recovers 98% of V28 from spent EV batteries using supercritical CO₂—profitably and at scale. Most strikingly, a 2025 breakthrough in China introduced a single-crystal V28-rich cathode that doubles cycle life to 5,000 charges, enabling million-mile EV batteries. Meanwhile, a Japanese team engineered a V28-based catalyst that splits seawater into hydrogen and oxygen with record efficiency, offering a low-cost path to green hydrogen without freshwater competition.

V28 has quietly greased the gears of industry for over a century. Now, with green mining, scalable battery recycling, and game-changing catalysts, it is earning its place as the workhorse of the clean-energy transition—still quiet, but now indispensable in ways our grandparents could never have imagined.
