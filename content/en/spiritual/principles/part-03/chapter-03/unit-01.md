## Buddhism as Jñāna Yoga: Liberation Through Wisdom and Insight

<!-- Across traditions, **Jñāna Yoga** is known as the path of **Wisdom and Direct Knowledge**—the supreme way to freedom through realizing the true nature of reality. The Buddha’s entire teaching can be summed in one sentence: **“Ignorance is the root of all suffering; wisdom is the path to freedom.”** He did not teach worship, sacrifice, or blind faith—he taught **investigation and realization**: question, observe, test, and see for yourself. This is pure Jñāna spirit.

The parallels are exact and profound:

### Inquiry and Self‑Examination

Jñāna Yoga asks: *“Who am I? What is real?”* Buddhism asks: *“Where there is no cause, there is no suffering. What is this ‘I’ that suffers?”* The Buddha refused to offer salvation through faith alone. He said: **“Do not accept anything out of respect for tradition or scripture. Test it as gold is tested—burn it, cut it, rub it—and only when you know it to be true, then accept it.”** That is the very voice of Jñāna Yoga.

### Seeing Through Illusion

Jñāna teaches that we suffer because we cling to what is impermanent and imagine a permanent “self” where none exists independently. Buddhism teaches exactly this: **the Three Marks of Existence**—*Anicca* (impermanence), *Dukkha* (suffering/unsatisfactoriness), *Anatta* (no separate self). To clearly see and understand these truths is the essence of wisdom. **You are not freeing yourself from something outside—you are freeing yourself from ignorance within.**

### Wisdom Is the Liberator

In Jñāna Yoga, knowledge is not information—it is **transformation**. You do not merely learn the truth; you become the truth. In Buddhism, this is **Prajñā**—transcendent wisdom that sees reality without veil or distortion. The Eightfold Path places **Right View and Understanding** first: all other practices—morality, meditation, mindfulness—exist solely to support and ripen wisdom. **Meditation is not the goal; meditation is the tool that makes wisdom visible.**
