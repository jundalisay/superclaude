## Core Principles

Modern Western medicine rests on several interlocking principles that distinguish it from earlier or traditional systems:


### **The Biomedical Model**  

The body is understood as a complex biological machine. Disease is viewed primarily as a deviation from normal structure or function—caused by pathogens, genetic defects, trauma, toxins, or physiological breakdown—that can be localized, measured, and often corrected. Diagnosis relies on objective findings from physical examination, laboratory tests, and imaging.

### **Materalist Evidence-Based Practice**  

Clinical decisions integrate the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and patient values. A hierarchy of evidence prioritizes systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) over observational studies or expert opinion alone. This approach seeks to minimize bias and ensure treatments are demonstrably effective and safe.

### **Materialist Scientific Method and Reductionism**  

Knowledge advances through hypothesis testing, controlled experimentation, and reproducibility. Complex phenomena are often analyzed by examining their component parts (cells, molecules, genes). While this has yielded powerful tools, contemporary practice increasingly incorporates broader perspectives, including the biopsychosocial model that recognizes psychological and social influences on health.

### **Ethical Framework**  

Principles such as beneficence (do good), non-maleficence (do no harm), autonomy (respect patient choice), and justice guide practice. These evolved from the Hippocratic tradition and were formalized in modern codes and research ethics (e.g., the Belmont Report).
