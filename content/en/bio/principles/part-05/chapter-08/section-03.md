Ayurveda translates roughly to the "Science of Life", from the Sanskrit *Ayur* meaning life, and *Veda* meaning knowledge.

It is not merely a medical system but a profound philosophical framework for harmonious living.

Ayurveda does not use the one-size-fits-all approach of modern conventional medicine.

Instead, it views health as a dynamic interplay between the individual, the environment, and the cosmos.


## History

According to myth, the knowledge of Ayurveda was first revealed by the creator god, Brahma, to the cosmic physicians, the Ashwini Kumaras.

They passed it to Indra, which we regard as Annunnaki, who then imparted the wisdom to the sage Bharadvaja. 

Bharadvaja, seeing humanity afflicted with disease, traveled to the heavens to learn this science and brought it down to Earth.

The golden age of Ayurveda, however, is historically associated with the great medical trinity of ancient India:

- **Charaka (circa 600 BCE):** The author of the *Charaka Samhita*, this text focuses heavily on internal medicine (*Kayachikitsa*). 

Charaka emphasized the role of digestion and metabolism, arguing that disease is a manifestation of imbalances within the body's internal environment.

- **Sushruta (circa 600–400 BCE):** The author of the *Sushruta Samhita*

Sushruta is often hailed as the "Father of Surgery." His text details over 300 surgical procedures, 120 surgical instruments, and an astonishingly detailed knowledge of human anatomy. He pioneered techniques like rhinoplasty (plastic surgery) and cataract surgery millennia before they emerged in the West.

- **Vagbhata (circa 400–500 CE):**  Synthesizing the works of his predecessors, Vagbhata authored the *Ashtanga Hridaya* and *Ashtanga Sangraha*. 

This text is considered the "Heart of Medicine" and is still widely used today due to its poetic brevity and clinical clarity.

For centuries, this knowledge was primarily an oral tradition, memorized by students and passed down through generations. However, during the Medieval period (circa 8th to 12th century CE), the Islamic invasions and the subsequent establishment of the Delhi Sultanate led to a decline in institutional support for Ayurveda. It was absorbed into the cultural melting pot of the subcontinent, influencing and being influenced by Unani (Greco-Arabic) medicine.


### The Colonial Decline and Modern Revival

British colonization dealt a severe blow to Ayurveda. 

In the 19th century, the British Raj favored Western allopathic medicine, dismissing Ayurveda as "unscientific" and "Hindu mysticism." 

It was stripped from the curriculum of Indian universities and pushed to the periphery. Yet, it survived in rural pockets. In the 20th century, a resurgence led by nationalists like Mahatma Gandhi, who saw it as a symbol of Swadeshi (self-reliance), revived its prestige.

Today, India has a dedicated Ministry of AYUSH (Ayurveda, Yoga, Unani, Siddha, and Homeopathy), and Ayurveda is a globally recognized complementary therapy.


## Principles

Ayurveda is based on the 5 Elements as **Pancha Mahabhutas**: 

- Akasha or Aether
- Vayu or Air or Chi
- Agni or Fire or Radiance
- Apas or Water
- Prithvi or Earth

These elements do not exist in isolation within the human body. They combine to form 3 biological humors, known as **Doshas**. These are the regulatory forces that govern all physiological and psychological processes.


| **Dosha** | **Elements** | **Nature** | **Function** | **In Balance** | **Imbalanced** |
| :---: | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Vata** | Space + Air | Kinetic, mobile, dry, cold, and light | Governs all movement—breathing, blinking, muscle movement, heartbeat, and nerve impulses | Creative, energetic, and flexible | Anxiety, insomnia, constipation, dry skin, and joint pain |
| **Pitta** | Fire + Water | Metabolic, hot, sharp, and oily | Governs digestion, absorption, assimilation, nutrition, metabolism, and body temperature | Intelligent, articulate, courageous, and good digestion | Ulcers, inflammation, anger, jealousy, rashes, and heartburn |
| **Kapha** | Earth + Water | Structural, heavy, slow, cool, and oily | Governs growth, strength, stability, immunity, and joint lubrication | Calm, loving, loyal, and strong | Lethargy, weight gain, sinus congestion, depression, and attachment |



Everyone possesses all three doshas, but the specific ratio present at the moment of conception determines your unique **Prakriti** (constitution). Your Prakriti is your "natural state"—your physical, mental, and emotional baseline.


### The Core Pillars of Ayurvedic Practice

Ayurvedic treatment is not about killing germs or removing tumors; it is about restoring balance. 

Disease (*Vikruti*) occurs when the doshas are aggravated by poor diet, stress, seasonal changes, or improper lifestyle. To restore health, Ayurveda relies on a "Tridoshic" approach, utilizing:

- **Ahara (Diet):** Food is medicine. Ayurveda categorizes food by its taste (*Rasa*)—sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter, and astringent. Each taste affects the doshas. For example, a Vata imbalance (dryness) is pacified by warm, oily, and sweet foods, while a Pitta imbalance (heat) is cooled by bitter and astringent foods.
- **Vihara (Lifestyle):** Your daily routine (*Dinacharya*) and seasonal routine (*Ritucharya*) are paramount. This includes waking up before sunrise, tongue scraping, oil pulling, self-massage with herbal oils (*Abhyanga*), and specific times for eating and sleeping.
- **Aushadha (Herbal Remedies):** Ayurveda boasts a vast pharmacopeia of approximately 2,000 herbs. Staples include *Triphala* (a three-fruit rejuvenator), *Ashwagandha* (an adaptogen for stress), *Turmeric* (an anti-inflammatory), and *Neem* (a blood purifier).
- **Panchakarma (The Five Actions):** This is Ayurveda's signature detoxification and rejuvenation therapy. It is a rigorous purification process involving medicated enemas, therapeutic vomiting, nasal administration of oils, and bloodletting (rarely used today). The goal is to expel deeply rooted toxins (*Ama*) from the tissues.
