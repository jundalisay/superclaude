Before the modern corporation, there was the guild.

In medieval Europe, guilds were more than trade associations. They were **self‑governing societies operating within the larger society**—complete with their own rules, hierarchies, courts, currencies, and moral codes.

A merchant or craftsman did not merely *join* a guild; he *entered* a parallel social order that governed his livelihood, his family, his obligations, and even his afterlife.

In this sense, the guild was the **precursor of the modern corporation**—but with one crucial difference: the guild was embedded in a web of social and spiritual duties, whereas the corporation is embedded in nothing but profit.


## The Guild as a Parallel Polity

A guild functioned as a **state within a state**:

| Feature | Guild | Modern Corporation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Internal laws | Statutes and ordinances | Bylaws, employee handbooks |
| Internal courts | Guild tribunals | HR departments, arbitration clauses |
| Currency | Sometimes issued its own tokens | Stock options, internal credits |
| Welfare | Supported widows, orphans, sick members | Benefits packages (optional) |
| Education | Apprentice → Journeyman → Master | Intern → Employee → Executive |
| Moral code | Religious and ethical obligations | CSR (corporate social responsibility) — optional |
| Loyalty | Lifetime, often hereditary | At‑will employment |

The guild was not just a workplace; it was a **community of fate**—members shared risk, reputation, and ritual.


## The Decline of Guilds

Guilds were replaced by private companies thanks to the rise of paper money. 

This allowed more loans to be handed out which then allowed more private people to do production themselves with a few employees. 

The creation of paper stock certificates allowed bigger companies, as corporations, that then allowed far bigger production than what any guild could do before. 

<!-- ## The Shift: From Duty to Contract -->

The modern corporation inherited the guild's structure—hierarchy, internal governance, exclusive membership—but **stripped away its obligations**.

Where the guild had duties to its members and to God, the corporation has duties only to shareholders. Where the guild was embedded in a moral economy, the corporation is a purely legal entity, free to relocate, downsize, or dissolve its "society" at will.

This is why the corporation feels hollow compared to the guild: it operates within society, but no longer *belongs* to it.
