We use the 4th Law of value for finance by matching:

- γ with the velocity of money in the Quantity Theory of Money
- k with Markup Pricing

Fourth Law: **V<sub>l4,t</sub> = k · P<sub>t</sub> · γ<sub>t</sub>**


1. **Velocity of money** (This is displacement)

Fisher's Equation of Exchange: **M • V = P • Q**

- M = money supply
- V = velocity of money (how fast money circulates)
- P = price level
- Q = quantity of real output (real GDP)


2. **Markup Pricing (Cost-Plus Pricing) Theory** (This is spin)

**P = MC × (1 + μ)** (or **P = MC / (1 - μ)**)

- P = price
- MC = marginal cost
- μ = markup ratio (over cost)

This is the standard microeconomics/industrial-organization model of firm pricing, used heavily in monopolistic competition and oligopoly theory (associated with economists like Kalecki, who built entire macro models around "markup pricing" as more realistic than pure competitive pricing). Your k plays the same structural role as μ — a multiplier applied to a "true" cost/value base to get the actual charged price.

## MICRO EXAMPLE: Pricing a Freelance Service

**Problem:** A freelance graphic designer wants to figure out what to actually charge a client, and why two clients might reasonably pay different amounts.

**Setup**

- **Pt (real productivity/value)** = the designer's real output — say, 10 hours of skilled work, benchmarked at a market-competitive labor rate of $50/hour = **$500** of real value created
- **x (fair exchange coefficient)** = the markup the designer applies as their "service fee" — reflecting expertise, reliability, overhead, and risk. Say the designer sets **x = 1.3** (a 30% markup over raw labor value) for a *known, repeat client* they trust
- **c (circulation coefficient)** = how healthy the payment flow is — is the client paying on time, in full, with working capital that keeps circulating? Assume **c = 1.0** (normal, healthy payment terms)

**Mt = 1.3 × 500 × 1.0 = $650**

**Now compare to a new, unknown client (higher risk, less trust — like the "farang price" mechanism):**

- Same Pt = $500 (same work)
- x = 1.6 (higher markup — designer prices in the risk/uncertainty of a new relationship, requires 50% deposit)
- c = 0.9 (slight undercirculation — client is notoriously slow-paying, so effective cash flow velocity is reduced even before the invoice is sent)

**Mt = 1.6 × 500 × 0.9 = $720**

**Takeaway:** Same real productivity (Pt), but different price (Mt) — driven by trust (x) *and* payment reliability (c). This is a legitimate micro-pricing insight: freelancers/small businesses often *should* price in both markup-for-risk (x) *and* a discount/premium for a client's payment behavior (c) — separately, not conflated into one number. Most freelancers only intuitively adjust x (their rate) and don't explicitly account for c (how fast/reliably that client's payments actually clear and let the freelancer reinvest) — the 4th Law framework forces you to separate those two levers.

## MACRO EXAMPLE: National Inflation Diagnosis

**Problem:** A central bank needs to diagnose why prices are rising — is it because businesses are gouging (x problem), or because there's too much money chasing the same goods (c problem)? This matters because the *policy response* is completely different depending on which one is broken.

**Setup — Using the equation of exchange analog (Mt=x•Pt•c ↔ P=μ•Q•V)**

Take a simplified national economy:

- **Pt (real output/GDP)** = $20 trillion (stable, say 2% real growth — not the driver of the problem)
- **x (markup)** = corporate profit margins — suppose margins expand from a historical average of 8% to **12%** post-pandemic (real, documented phenomenon — often called "greedflation" in the 2021-2023 debate)
- **c (velocity/circulation)** = M2 money velocity — during COVID, this actually *fell* sharply (people held cash, didn't spend), then partially recovered as stimulus was spent down

**Scenario A: Pre-pandemic baseline**
- x = 1.08, Pt = $20T, c = 1.0 (normal velocity)
- Mt (nominal price level proxy) = 1.08 × 20 × 1.0 = **21.6** (index)

**Scenario B: 2021-2022 stimulus period**
- x = 1.12 (margin expansion — corporations raised prices beyond input cost increases)
- Pt = $20.4T (modest real growth)
- c = 1.15 (overcirculation — trillions in stimulus checks + PPP loans hit the economy while output hadn't caught up, plus supply chain constraints meant the same goods were being chased by more dollars)

Mt = 1.12 × 20.4 × 1.15 = **26.3** (index) — a **~22% nominal price increase**

**Diagnostic value of separating x and c:**

This is the actually useful part for real policy: economists and journalists spent 2021-2023 arguing about exactly this decomposition —
- **If it's mostly a c problem** (excess money/stimulus + supply shocks) → the fix is tightening monetary policy (higher interest rates, reduce money supply) — this is what the Fed actually did
- **If it's mostly an x problem** (corporate margin expansion / price-gouging) → the fix would be different: antitrust action, price controls, or public pressure on markup behavior — this was the "greedflation" political argument (raised by some economists and politicians, contested by others)

Real data roughly supports **both being partially true** — Fed research (San Francisco Fed, 2023) found corporate margins did contribute meaningfully to 2021-22 inflation, while velocity/money supply effects (excess stimulus relative to output) also contributed. The 4th Law equation is useful here precisely because it forces you to **not conflate these two separate causes** into a single "inflation is bad" diagnosis — which is exactly the same structural point your song's verse 2 makes ("it ain't just the markup, it ain't just the make, it's the speed of the river").

---

**Why this framework has real utility beyond the metaphor:**

Both examples show the same practical value: **decomposing a price into "fair-exchange" component vs. "circulation" component lets you diagnose the actual cause of a pricing problem** (whether it's a $70 invoice or a 9% CPI print) rather than treating "price went up" as one undifferentiated phenomenon. That decomposition — x vs. c — maps cleanly onto real, actively-debated economic questions (markup/margin-driven inflation vs. velocity/money-supply-driven inflation), which is a legitimate contribution of the framework, independent of whether you accept its more speculative claims (thermodynamic derivation, "economy becoming united," etc.).
