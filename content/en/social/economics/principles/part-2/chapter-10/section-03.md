This is an economic system ruled by the armed forces (or military-backed leaders).

It typically suffers from:
- overregulation which violates Law 3
- overcapitalization of the military which violates Law 2
- undercirculation which violates Law 1

It often persists because it focuses on Law 1, except in North Korea where the people have starved.

Examples are:
- Nazi Germany
- Imperial Japan during WWII
- Fascist Italy
- African dictatorships
- North Korea



<!-- ### Egypt under Abdel Fattah el-Sisi
Since the 2013 military ouster of Mohamed Morsi and Sisi’s subsequent rise (president since 2014, with terms extended via 2019 constitutional changes allowing rule until at least 2030), Egypt has operated as a military-backed authoritarian system. The armed forces play a central economic role beyond defense. Military-owned or affiliated companies dominate or hold privileged positions in construction, real estate, agriculture, manufacturing, cement, steel, food production, and infrastructure megaprojects (such as the New Administrative Capital and Suez Canal expansion).

These entities often enjoy tax exemptions, preferential access to land and credit, cheap conscript labor, and opacity, crowding out private firms and contributing to an uneven playing field. Analysts and the IMF have linked this “military landlord” or state-capitalist model to structural problems: low private investment (averaging around 6% of GDP in recent years), high public debt, inflation spikes, and reliance on foreign loans/grants (including large IMF packages and Gulf investments). Growth has occurred in some periods via public spending, but productivity outside rentier and construction sectors has stagnated, with rising poverty and inequality reported. The model prioritizes regime-linked projects and control over market-driven efficiency.

### North Korea under the Kim dynasty
North Korea is a totalitarian hereditary dictatorship with a strong military (Songun, or “military-first”) orientation. The Korean People’s Army is enormous relative to population, and military spending is estimated at a very high share of GDP (often cited in the 20–30%+ range by external analysts). The official economy is a rigid command system focused on heavy industry, defense production, and self-reliance (Juche), with severe isolation and sanctions.

In practice, the regime extracts resources through state control, forced labor (domestic and overseas workers whose earnings largely go to the state), illicit activities (cyber theft, arms exports, smuggling), and limited informal markets that emerged after the 1990s famine. Recent years have seen revenue boosts from arms and troop support to Russia amid the Ukraine war, plus Chinese trade, enabling some growth (Bank of Korea estimates of ~3%+ in recent years) and showcase projects in Pyongyang. However, resources remain heavily directed toward the military, nuclear/missile programs, elite consumption, and regime security rather than broad living standards. Chronic shortages, rationing tied to loyalty, and suppression of independent economic activity persist. The system prioritizes regime survival and military power over consumer welfare or market efficiency.

### Iraq under Saddam Hussein (1979–2003)
Saddam’s Ba’athist regime was a highly personalistic dictatorship with deep military and security-service control (though not a pure junta). The economy was a classic oil rentier command system: nationalized oil revenues funded state-led industrialization, infrastructure, education, and a welfare apparatus in the 1970s boom years, when Iraq achieved relatively high regional living standards and middle-income status.

Military spending soared (especially during the 1980–88 Iran-Iraq War), absorbing a large share of resources and labor. Central planning, subsidies, and restrictions on the private sector created inefficiencies. Wars, debt, the 1990 invasion of Kuwait, and subsequent UN sanctions caused collapse: GDP per capita plummeted, the middle class was impoverished, and the regime relied on smuggling, illicit oil sales, and patronage to survive. Non-oil sectors atrophied; the economy became even more extractive and militarized. Outcomes shifted from 1970s expansion (driven by oil prices and state investment) to prolonged decline under militarism, isolation, and repression.

### Shared patterns and limits
Across these cases, military or military-backed dictatorships tend to treat the economy as an instrument of political control: privileging loyal networks, funding large security apparatuses, and limiting autonomous private power that could challenge the regime. Growth can occur (especially with resource rents or external support), but it is often uneven, debt- or patronage-dependent, and vulnerable to isolation, corruption, or policy missteps. Private-sector dynamism, innovation, and broad prosperity are frequently secondary to regime security. Historical “developmental dictatorships” elsewhere sometimes delivered industrialization under authoritarian rule, but the examples above illustrate the risks of prioritising military and elite interests over open, competitive economic institutions.

Military dictatorship is ultimately a method of political rule with economic consequences, not a coherent alternative economic system like market capitalism or central planning in pure form. Its performance depends heavily on context, resources, and the specific choices of the rulers. -->
